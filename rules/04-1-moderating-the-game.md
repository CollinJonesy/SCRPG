# Moderating the Game (Ch. 4) — Reference
Source: printed pg.145–180. Paraphrased and reorganized from the full chapter — not the book's original text.

## Elements of a Scene

A published (or self-built) scene is made of: the **Scene Tracker**, opposing **Villains/Lieutenants/Minions**, an **Environment/Location**, pre-made **Challenges**, and a curated list of **Twists** tuned to that scene/environment. Chapter 5 (The Bullpen) covers building your own from scratch.

## Scene GYRO & the Scene Tracker

The scene tracker starts Green; each space checked moves it toward Red, then Out. It gets its own "turn" in the action order — check the next space, and if an environment is present, the environment also acts on that turn. When the tracker runs out of spaces, something bad happens (villain's plan advances, a rescue fails, a portal grows) and play usually rolls straight into a new, higher-stakes scene.

**Whichever status (personal or scene) is closer to Out wins** for determining ability access, status die, and what threats/twists the environment can pull from — except environment twists specifically, which only ever look at the *scene's* status, never a hero's personal one.

**Very simple scenes** can skip the tracker entirely — just assume Green throughout, and end the scene when the opposition/challenges are cleared or there's nothing left worth complicating.

## Who's Who in a Scene (NPC categories)

- **Bystanders** — no stats; represent them as challenges (needing rescue/protection) or as a source of Hinder against the heroes.
- **Allied Minions** — minions temporarily helping the heroes.
- **Minions / Lieutenants / Villains** — see Ch.2's save mechanics; full creation rules are in Ch.5.
- **Plot characters** and **Threats** — whatever the story needs; recurring NPCs can shift categories scene to scene, whatever's easiest to run.

## Running the Action Order

SCRPG uses an **elective initiative** system — the *current* actor picks who goes next, not a fixed order. As GM you're tracking every non-hero element (villains, minions, environment, scene tracker).

Practical tips from the book:
- **Group same-type minions on one index card** so they all act in a block rather than needing separate bookkeeping — e.g. one card for "6 Spiderbots," one for "4 Alien Soldiers."
- Use tokens/beads/sideways cards to mark who's already acted this round.
- Pull a card from the table entirely once it's no longer relevant (challenge resolved, villain defeated) — the shrinking pile of cards visibly signals the heroes are winning.
- **If the heroes all go first in a round**, expect them to try passing "who goes next" among their own opposition at the end of the round so those enemies get two turns back-to-back across the round boundary — this is a known elective-initiative tactic hostile NPCs can exploit right back.
- **Initiative Anywhere**: the action order isn't just for action scenes — layer it onto a social scene if pacing needs it or if some players are dominating the table.

## Ganging Up (fairness guideline)

Don't let a pile of enemies dogpile one vulnerable hero unless a player specifically asks for that spotlight. Spread threats across the team, or focus on heroes who benefit narratively from taking damage. If a villain deliberately wants to isolate one hero, telegraph it clearly (monologue, shouted orders) rather than doing it silently.

## Handling Twists in Play

Priority order for picking a twist: (1) check if something on the affected hero's own sheet (their Principle-linked prompts) fits, (2) fall back to twists tied to the environment or written for the scene, (3) improvise your own — see "Creating Twists," Ch.5 pg.200. Always frame *why* the twist is happening in-fiction; twists that feel arbitrary land worse than ones tied to what's actually happening.

Minor twist effects generally clear at scene end; major twist effects generally clear at issue end — mods can be cancelled early by an opposing Boost/Hinder, and disabled traits can usually be restored by a fitting Overcome.

## Timed Challenges (GM-running notes)

A Timed Challenge has its own timer track and takes its own turn in the action order, checking off a timer box each time (parallel to how the scene tracker gets checked). If the last timer box fills before the challenge is resolved, its "triggered" consequence fires immediately. Good timed-challenge material: bombs, falls, crashing vehicles, hostage stand-offs, collapsing structures, gas leaks — anything that punishes waiting.

## Doomsday Devices (GM-running notes)

Mechanically, a Doomsday Device is a Challenge with a timer that **also accelerates the Scene Tracker on its own turn** — some check one space, some two, some jump straight to the next zone or even straight to Red. If a Doomsday Device is active, running out the Scene Tracker means it actually goes off. Always read the specific device's write-up for its exact behavior; see Ch.5 for building your own.

## Session Structure

- **Elements of a Scene** → **The GM's Turn** → run Action/Social/Montage scenes as needed → **End of Session Wrap-Up** (naming the issue, updating Back Issues/Collections).
- Chapter 5 (The Bullpen) is where you build your *own* scenes, challenges, twists, minions, lieutenants, villains, and environments from scratch rather than running pre-made ones.

## The GM's Principles

Three headline ideas the book asks every GM to hold onto:

### 1. Make the rules a way to deliver fun
- **Teach the rules** patiently — some players will master the system, others just want to roll dice with friends, and both are valid.
- **Be fair** — apply rules evenly, don't let rules-savvy players (including yourself) steamroll others, make sure everyone gets pulled into the conversation if they're being talked over.
- **Share the spotlight** — use the action order deliberately (even in social scenes) if some players are dominating; every player has final say over their own hero's actions.
- **"There's no rule for that?"** — treat Overcome as your default catch-all for "can I do this?", borrow a similar existing rule if one fits, use a twist as the drawback if you want to say yes with a cost, or just say **"Yes."** Ask "what's the most fun outcome right now?" rather than freezing up over an edge case.
- **Make every roll meaningful** — heroes are already competent at their own specialties; don't ask for a roll on something their powers/qualities/principles already establish as a given (Legacy lifting a car isn't in question). Reach for dice when the outcome is genuinely uncertain, high-stakes, or the hero is outside their wheelhouse.
- **"Yes" is the GM's best friend** — if a request fits the spirit of the game and especially a hero's Principles, you can just narrate success and move on; not everything needs a failure chance.
- **Which die matters** — beyond raw die size, *which* power/quality gets used should make narrative sense and shapes what twist would fit if one comes up (Strength vs. Telekinesis busting a door implies different complications).

### 2. Drive play toward hard choices and sacrifice
- **Villains are committed to their beliefs** — give them real motivations (greed, revenge, glory, hatred, old trauma) and let them monologue in bite-sized pieces during their turns, since players will rarely sit still for a long speech.
- **Twists are your best tool** for injecting new story material — encourage heroes to lean into them (callbacks to past issues, origin elements, story-arc themes) rather than treating them as pure punishment. Not every twist needs to be huge; save the big swings for when the moment earns it.
- **A hero is willing to sacrifice** — Principle-linked twists exist so a player can choose to expose something about their hero rather than fail or escalate danger. If a player doesn't want to use their own twist, you supply the complication instead — ideally after checking in with the table.
- **Heroes are front and center** — stack visible challenges (index cards, minis, whatever) so the scene *looks* overwhelming at a glance; that's what makes "and THEN—" landings hit.
- **A bit of love for the helpers** — support-focused heroes (Boost/Hinder specialists) can feel like they're doing less; reward that playstyle with extra scene information, clues, or secret notes tied to their support actions so it still feels meaningful.
- **Keep hope alive** — even while ramping difficulty, make sure the table never feels like the fight is unwinnable. Spread damage across the team, have excess minions Boost/Defend/Hinder instead of piling on Attacks, and use twists to raise tension back up if things are going too smoothly instead of just adding more enemies.
