# Building Villains


Guidance for putting together minions, lieutenants, and full villains from scratch. Source: *Sentinel Comics RPG* core rulebook, "The Bullpen" chapter, pp. 204–239.


See also: [[13_Minions_Lieutenants_and_Environments]] and [[21_Archives_Villains]].


---


## Creating Minions


Minions are simple, low-powered threats meant to chip away at hero Health during an action scene — but the framework is flexible enough to represent anything from a masked bank robber to a starcruiser crewed by aliens.


Building a minion is a short checklist:


- Name the minion and settle on its concept.
- Give it a single die to represent its power.
- Write a short description of who or what it is and what it can do.
- *Optional:* add one or two special abilities to make it stand out.
- *Optional:* jot down a line of tactics as a reminder for running it in play.


### Minion Die Size


Once you know roughly what the minion is, pick its die size. If you're building a minion for a single scene, the Scene Element Difficulty quick-reference chart (p. 186) can guide the choice. As a rough set of examples by die size, the rulebook offers a tier of inspirations ranging from the weakest to the toughest:


- **Weakest tier** — damaged bots, a panicked mob, rat swarms, untrained humans, ravenous guppies.
- **Next tier** — armed thugs, police officers, security guards, street fighters, piranhas.
- **Mid tier** — cyborg guards, ninjas, professional soldiers, raptors, sharks.
- **Tougher tier** — battle-armored soldiers, elite assassins, elite cyborg commandos, laser sharks.
- **Toughest tier** — armored infantry carriers, enraged T-rexes, gunships, megalodons.


The source lists these as flavor examples tied to an ascending die-size progression rather than spelling out exact die values for each tier — use them as relative benchmarks, not fixed numbers.


Minions with high dice get dangerous fast in numbers. If you want more capable, specialized minions without risking a one-sided wipeout, favor giving them small situational bonuses (rule of thumb: value 2, kept in the range of 1–3) over simply raising their die size.


### Adding Abilities


Minions don't need abilities — most just deal damage and get pummeled. If you do give them one or two, common options include:


- A situational bonus to a basic action under specific conditions
- A bonus to Boost (for an ally) or Hinder (against an enemy) actions
- A bonus to Defend actions targeting one ally
- A bonus to damage or to save
- The ability to Attack an additional target, usually at a penalty
- Free movement between locations without spending an action
- The capacity to sacrifice itself to: prevent damage to a villain, Attack all enemies in its location (at a penalty), Boost all allies in its location, penalize all enemies in the environment, recover a villain's Health equal to a die roll, bring an environment threat/twist into play, or split into minions of a smaller die size


### Minion Tactics


Mostly seen in published material: a sentence or two describing how a minion type behaves in an action scene, useful if you've built up a roster of homegrown minions in advance.

## Creating Lieutenants


Lieutenants use the same single-die structure as minions but are harder to knock out and carry more and better abilities. They can represent a villain's elite followers (including superpowered underlings) or non-humanoid threats — large dinosaurs, war engines, a gun-nested flamethrower tractor rig, and the like.


Build a lieutenant the same way as a minion, with these differences:


### Naming


Lieutenants tend to be unique or at least distinctive. Give individuals a full name and, often, a title — e.g. "Ember Shadow, Ninja Queen," "Admiral Zrill, Ur-Commander of the 17th Galactic Fleet," "XL78, Rogue Warbot."


### Lieutenant Die Size


Lieutenants are most commonly ranked somewhere in the **d4-to-d10 range** — the exact top end isn't pinned to a single number in the source, so treat this as a range rather than a fixed ceiling. Their die reflects how massive, armored, or skillful they are. Because a lieutenant doesn't degrade a die size on a successful save against an Attack (unlike a minion), they're inherently hardier than minions of the same die.


A **d6 lieutenant is called out as "more rare"** in the source — not a standard default. It fits a threat that isn't especially deadly but is hard to put down and keeps coming back, such as a swarm of insects pouring out of a sewer. Do not treat d6 as the baseline; the source gives a range starting at d4, with d6 flagged specifically as the unusual case.


### Massive-Scale Opponents


Lieutenants aren't locked to any size scale, but should still be threats heroes can realistically fight in a single action scene. For truly colossal foes (a skyscraper-sized statue, a sentient flying island), represent them instead as a combination of an environment, several challenges to "beat," and threats that try to stop the heroes from succeeding — rather than as one oversized lieutenant.


### Adding Abilities


Lieutenants should get one or more abilities. Pull from the minion ability list above, or from lieutenant-scale options such as:


- A bonus to two or more basic actions under specific conditions
- A bonus to Boost, Hinder, or Defend actions — including versions that hit multiple allies/enemies at once
- Combining an Attack or Defend with a Boost or Hinder on the same die roll
- A special action: create minions based on a die roll, recover a villain's Health by a die roll, bring an environment threat/twist into play, or move a hero to another location
- The capacity to sacrifice itself to: let the villain escape, let the villain act during the lieutenant's turn, or advance the scene tracker by one space

## Creating Villains


Villain creation sits between hero creation and the simpler scene-element rules. Like heroes, villains have powers, qualities, a status die (which often behaves differently than a hero's), Health, and abilities.


A **minor villain** uses just their base stats — for a brief appearance, working alongside other villains, or when prolonged combat isn't the point. A **full villain** has the same stats plus one or more upgrades that make them a real threat to an entire team of heroes.


### The Process


1. Come up with a concept.
2. Choose the villain's **Approach** — their core stats and how they pursue their villainous goals in a scene.
3. Assign powers and qualities from the standard lists.
4. Assign abilities from the chosen Approach.
5. Choose the villain's **Archetype** — how they generally operate, what drives them, and what they care about in a scene.
6. Gain status dice from the Archetype.
7. Assign abilities from the Archetype.
8. *Optional:* add one or more Upgrades.
9. *Optional:* add a Villainous Mastery.
10. Calculate total Health (Approach + Archetype + number-of-heroes×5 + any Upgrades).
11. Add finishing touches (appearance, personality, motivation).


**Approach and Archetype are two separate, independent choices** — combining one of each is what produces a complete villain. The rulebook is explicit that these are guidelines, not hard restrictions: a recurring villain can swap Approach or Archetype between appearances if the story calls for it.


### Concept


Before the mechanics, settle on who the villain is — a brilliant inventor hungry for power with an army of robotic followers, a mind-burglar who turns heroes against each other, an unknowable force of chaos with unclear motives. The concept can keep shifting through the build process as it meets the mechanics.


### Villain Approaches


A villain's **Approach** determines how they go about achieving their goals — it grants suggested powers, suggested qualities, a base Health value, and its own pool of abilities. Every villain also gets a roleplaying quality regardless of Approach.
