# GM Basics: Scenes, Turns, and Villains


Source: `Rulebook MD/Ch04_moderating_the_game.md`, lines ~558–1315.


This page covers the core toolkit a GM uses moment-to-moment: what a scene is
made of, how the GM's turn works, how to read a villain sheet, and what
villains are allowed to do on their turn. For how minions, lieutenants, and
environments actually run in play, see
[[13_Minions_Lieutenants_and_Environments]]. For the step-by-step process of
setting up and running full scenes, see [[14_Running_Scenes]].


## Elements of a Scene


Every scene is built from a handful of recurring pieces that the GM combines
to create tension and give players something to interact with:


- **Scene tracker** — Tracks the danger/tension of the scene using GYRO
  (Green → Yellow → Red → Out). It gets its own turn in the initiative order,
  during which it checks off one space.
- **Villains / lieutenants / minions** — The NPCs opposing the heroes. They
  may start in the scene or arrive as it unfolds.
- **Environment / location** — The setting of the scene and what it
  mechanically contributes to the action.
- **Challenges** — Pre-built structured problems for the heroes to resolve
  (not covered in depth on this page).
- **Twists** — Minor and major twist lists tuned to the scene and its
  environment.


A published issue supplies most of these ready-made, but a GM can also build
their own scenes, issues, and even whole collections from scratch (see
[[16_Building_Villains]] and the Bullpen material for custom content).


## The GM's Turn


When play passes control to the GM — whether that's the scene tracker,
an environment, a villain, or a minion — here's what's actually happening
mechanically.


### The Scene Tracker and GYRO Status


The scene tracker measures the danger, intensity, and desperation of an
action scene. It starts in the **Green** zone. When the last Green space is
checked, the scene's status becomes **Yellow**; when the last Yellow space is
checked, it becomes **Red**. When the final Red space is marked, the scene is
**Out** — something bad happens (the villain's plan advances, a rescue fails,
a threat grows worse) and the scene ends, often leading into a new scene with
higher stakes.


**Which status applies — the "closer to Out" rule:**
Both the scene itself and each individual hero can have a GYRO status. When
a hero's personal status and the scene's status differ, **use whichever one
is closer to Out.**


- If the scene is Yellow, every hero is treated as *at least* Yellow — even
  a hero whose personal status is still Green.
- If a hero's personal status is Red, that hero is still Red even if the
  scene itself is only Yellow.


Characters have access to abilities in their current zone **and** any less
dire zone: a Green-status character can typically only use Green abilities;
a Yellow-status character can use Green and Yellow; a Red-status character
can use Green, Yellow, and Red. An **Out** character may only use their Out
ability — they're severely limited but can still contribute in a way
specific to their character.


Environment threats and twists unlock the same way (by GYRO zone), but they
look **only** at the scene's status — not any individual hero's or villain's
personal status.


A very simple action scene can skip the scene tracker entirely; in that case
assume the scene's status stays Green throughout, and the scene ends when all
challenges/opponents are dealt with or no interesting complication remains.


### When to Check a Space


Most of the time, the scene tracker checks its next space on **its own
turn** — it gets a turn in the initiative order just like any NPC. If the
scene has an environment, the environment acts on the scene tracker's turn
too: check the next space first, then run the environment's actions (see
[[13_Minions_Lieutenants_and_Environments]] for the full three-step
sequence). Either way, the GM picks the next actor in the initiative as if
the scene tracker/environment were an NPC.


### Who's Who in Scenes


The GM plays every non-hero character. These fall into several categories,
and a single character can shift categories from scene to scene if that's
more useful for play:


- **Bystanders** — Background civilians with no stats. Used to complicate
  action scenes (needing rescue, getting in the way); represented as
  challenges or as sources of Hinder effects, not as statted characters.
- **Allied minions** — NPCs who provide meaningful help (police, soldiers,
  professionals). Their turns can be handed to a player to control.
- **Minions** — Nameless/faceless rank-and-file opposition. See
  [[13_Minions_Lieutenants_and_Environments]] for their mechanics.
- **Lieutenants** — Tougher recurring henchmen, vehicles, or similar. See
  [[13_Minions_Lieutenants_and_Environments]].
- **Villains** — The main antagonists; full stat blocks, covered below.
- **Plot characters** — Named NPCs who advance the story but don't always
  act in action scenes. May be statted like heroes, villains, or
  minions/lieutenants depending on their role.
- **Threats** — Hostile minions/lieutenants/villains not necessarily present
  at the start of a scene, introduced later via twists, environment actions,
  or other characters' actions.


## An Illustrated Guide to the Villain


The villain sheet is the GM's reference for running a villain in play. Its
main elements:


1. **Villain Name** — Their villainous moniker.
2. **Alias** — The villain's given name.
3. **Approach** — How this villain confronts obstacles, set during villain
   creation.
4. **Archetype** — The category of villain they are, also set during villain
   creation.
5. **Villain Picture** — Art of the villain.
6. **Health / Current Health** — Maximum health and a tracker for current
   health as it changes in play.
7. **Powers and Qualities** — Like heroes, villains draw on powers and
   qualities in both combat and social conflict.
8. **Status** — A villain's GYRO-like status track is defined by their
   archetype and does **not** necessarily track their health the way a
   hero's status does. (Example: Baron Blade's status die is based on how
   many Invention mods he has active, not his remaining Health.)
9. **Abilities** — Signature moves that let villains use basic actions in
   distinctive ways.
10. **Upgrades/Masteries** — Additional stat elements some villains have,
    often giving heroes an alternate way to dismantle the villain (e.g.
    disabling powered armor via repeated Overcome actions or enough damage).


### Important: Villain Abilities Are Not Zone-Gated


Unlike heroes, whose available abilities depend on their current GYRO zone,
**villain abilities are not restricted by status/zone at all.** The source
text states this explicitly:


> "villain abilities are not based on the zone they are in. Rather, they
> always have access to all of their abilities."


In other words, a villain can use any of their listed abilities on any turn,
regardless of the scene's or their own GYRO status — this is a deliberate
asymmetry between hero and villain design.


## What Villains Do


On a villain's turn the GM has them either take a basic action or use one of
their abilities. (Some villains have special rules letting them take
multiple actions — check the individual villain's write-up.)


### Take a Basic Action


Villains can take any of the five basic actions — Attack, Defend, Boost,
Hinder, and Overcome — assembling a dice pool from their powers, qualities,
and status die (drawn from their archetype), same as heroes, with special
notes on Overcome below.


### Use an Ability


A villain's abilities are typically more potent than a hero's and shape what
kind of plots, schemes, and tactics they bring to a scene. Their archetype
informs the flavor of these abilities (e.g. an "Overlord" archetype leans
into empowering and replenishing their own minions).


### Overcome


Villains Overcome obstacles similarly to heroes. Major villains often have
masteries letting them auto-succeed at Overcome checks within their area of
expertise. Since SCRPG has no opposed rolls, a villain can't use Overcome to
directly cancel a hero's action — that's what Hinder or a villain's special
ability is for.


**Overcome to advance the scene tracker:** A villain can also use an
Overcome action to make the scene more dangerous — causing chaos, delivering
a monologue, etc. On a success, the scene tracker advances one space.


⚠️ **Rule as written, verify die value used:** the source text marks this
Overcome check with a d6 icon in context, but does not spell out that the
target number is different from a normal Overcome — treat this as a normal
Overcome action whose *effect on success* is advancing the tracker. Needs
physical-book confirmation if a specific fixed die/target is intended beyond
the villain's normal dice pool.


**Limit: once per scene, at most.** This is called out explicitly as
important — overusing this to advance the tracker can derail pacing and feel
unfair to players, especially if they aren't aware the GM has this option.


### Villains and Minor Twists


Villains can succeed with minor twists, using a villain-specific list rather
than the general hero twist list:


- Villain takes damage equal to their Max die.
- Villain eliminates one of their own minions, or lowers the die size of one
  of their lieutenants.
- Villain takes a Hinder-style penalty, or grants a hero in the same
  location a Boost-style bonus, equal to their Max die.
- Villain inflicts a Mid-die Hinder penalty on all their own minions and
  lieutenants, or grants the heroes a Mid-die Boost bonus.
- Villain skips their next action to deal with a consequence of their own
  action.


These twists generally have an immediate, scene-local effect rather than an
ongoing story effect (unlike hero major twists).


### Major Twists for Villains


**Villains should not take major twists.** If a villain would otherwise
succeed with a major twist, treat it as a **failure** instead — major twists
are designed to follow a hero around for a full issue, and villains
typically aren't present that long.


**The one exception:** a villain's major twist can be used to **end the
scene immediately** — e.g. the villain is captured mid-scheme, or their plan
fails outright but they escape to return later. This is a narrative tool for
when a scene is running long or a dramatic beat would serve the story.
